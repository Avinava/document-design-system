# Deck structure

Slide-level craft does not save a deck with no argument. Structure first.

## Start from the ask

Before writing slide one, answer: **what should be different after this deck?**

- A decision made
- A budget approved
- A risk understood and accepted
- A plan aligned on
- Information transferred, with nothing required in return

If the answer is the last one, and nothing is being asked for, consider whether this should be a document instead. Documents are better at transferring information and do not require anybody's calendar.

Everything in the deck either supports the ask or comes out.

## The arc

Most decks that work follow the same shape:

1. **Context** — what the audience needs to hold in mind. One or two slides, not five. Assume they know their own business.
2. **The finding** — the thing they do not already know. Get here fast; a deck that reaches its point on slide twelve has lost the room.
3. **The evidence** — why the finding is true. The bulk of the deck, and where charts and diagrams live.
4. **The implication** — what it means for them specifically.
5. **The ask** — what happens next, who owns it, by when.

Two common inversions, both worth resisting:

**Building to the finding.** Chronological reconstruction of the analysis — how you gathered the data, what you tried, what you concluded. That is the order you discovered it, not the order the audience needs it. Lead with the conclusion.

**Context that never ends.** Five slides of background before the first new information. The audience already knows their business; they came for what you found.

## Length

Roughly one to two minutes per slide when presented. A 30-minute slot is 15–25 slides, and fewer is almost always better.

Decks meant to be read can be longer, because the reader controls the pace — but a read deck over about 40 slides should probably be a document.

**The cut test:** if a slide were removed, would the argument still land? If yes, remove it. Do this once after the deck is complete; almost every deck loses a fifth of its slides and improves.

## Appendix

Real appendices are useful — detail you do not present but want available when someone asks.

- Put them after a clearly marked divider.
- Do not reference them in the main flow. If it needs presenting, it is not an appendix.
- The main deck must stand alone without them.

An appendix is not a place to hide slides you could not bring yourself to cut.

## Consistency

A deck is one artifact. Across every slide:

- Titles sit in the same position, at the same size.
- Body text sits on the same baseline.
- Charts use the same scales where they show the same measure. Two charts of the same quantity on different scales is misleading, whether or not it is intended.
- Terminology is identical throughout. If it is "ingestion" on slide 4, it is not "intake" on slide 9.
- Number formatting matches everywhere — same units, same precision, same thousands separator.

Inconsistency reads as carelessness, and an audience that notices carelessness in the formatting starts wondering about the analysis.

## Handover

When delivering, state:

- Whether the deck is meant to be presented or read.
- The PDF export path, and that "Background graphics" must be enabled if the deck uses them.
- Any accompanying document holding the detail the deck deliberately omits.
- The as-of date of the data.
